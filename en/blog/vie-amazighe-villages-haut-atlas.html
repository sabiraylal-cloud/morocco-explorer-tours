Inside the Amazigh villages of the High Atlas: daily life and hospitality

Before it is a hiking backdrop, the High Atlas is a lived-in land. In every valley, Amazigh families farm the terraces, raise their animals and have welcomed travellers for generations. Understanding a little of their daily life completely changes the way you walk in these mountains.

Who are the Amazigh people of the High Atlas?

The Amazigh, often called Berbers, are the first inhabitants of North Africa. The word Imazighen, the plural of Amazigh, is often translated as “free people”. In the High Atlas around Marrakech, the everyday language is Tachelhit, one of the Amazigh languages.

Since 2011, Amazigh has been an official language of Morocco alongside Arabic. You will see its alphabet, Tifinagh, on some signs and public buildings. In the villages many people also speak Moroccan Arabic, Darija; French is mostly spoken by guides and younger people.

A day in the village

Life follows the seasons. On the terraces, watered by small channels called seguias, families grow barley, maize and vegetables, and the trees give walnuts and apples. Every drop of water is shared between families according to long-standing rules.

Sheep and goats go out onto the slopes every day. In summer, part of the flocks moves up to high pastures managed collectively under a system called agdal, which protects the grass until a date set by the community. The mule is still essential: it carries wood, crops and, on our treks, your luggage.

Houses of earth and stone

Traditional houses are built of stone and rammed earth, with wooden beams and flat roofs. The roofs are used to dry the harvest, and the ground floor often shelters animals and stores. Villages climb the mountainside to keep the flat land for crops.

In September 2023, a powerful earthquake struck the Al Haouz region, and many High Atlas villages were badly hit. Rebuilding is still under way. Travelling with local guides, muleteers and hosts is also a direct way to support the families of these valleys.

At the table: bread, tagine and mint tea

Bread is baked every day, often in a clay oven, and comes with every meal. Tagine simmers slowly over embers: vegetables, meat or chicken, olives and spices. Couscous is traditionally served on Fridays. Depending on the valley, you may also be offered honey, olive oil or walnuts.

Mint tea is the heart of hospitality: green tea, fresh mint and plenty of sugar, poured from high up to make a foam. People often eat from a shared dish, using the right hand and a piece of bread. If you prefer less sugar, just ask for “bla sukkar”, without sugar.

Being a respectful guest

A few words always bring a smile: “Salam alikoum” to say hello, “Azul” in Amazigh, and “Tanmirt” to say thank you in Tachelhit. Take your shoes off when entering a home, accept the tea you are offered and taste at least a little of every dish.

In the villages, clothes that cover shoulders and knees are appreciated, for women and men alike. Always ask before photographing someone, especially women and children, and accept a no with a smile. Avoid giving sweets or money to children, as it encourages begging. If you would like to help, talk to your guide, who can direct your support to the school or a cooperative.

What your visit brings to the valleys

Mountain tourism supports many families: guides, muleteers, gîtes, cooks and cooperatives. A night in a family home, as on our trek in the Oued Zat valley, is one of the best ways to share this daily life. For a first taste, a day in Imlil already takes you through several villages and includes tea in a Berber home.

Good habits as a guest

  • Take your shoes off before entering and sit where you are invited to.
  • Eat with your right hand and taste everything, even just a little.
  • Ask before taking photos, and never photograph inside a home without permission.
  • Cover shoulders and knees in the villages; a light scarf is always useful.
  • Buy directly from cooperatives rather than giving to children.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to speak Tachelhit or Arabic?

No. Your guide translates, and a few words like “Azul” or “Tanmirt” are enough to make people smile.

Can I stay with a local family?

Yes. Our trek in the Oued Zat valley is spent entirely with families or in camp. Comfort is simple and the welcome is warm.

What if I don’t eat meat?

Tell us in advance: vegetable tagines, omelettes, lentils and salads are easy to prepare in the mountains.
