Off the beaten path: the Oued Zat valley, Tlatast and the Yagour engravings

Everyone knows Imlil and Toubkal. Far fewer travellers have heard of the Oued Zat. Yet it is one of the valleys I most love to share: villages that still live by the seasons, potters at work and, on a high plateau, engravings left by people several thousand years ago.

A valley away from the crowds

The Oued Zat is a High Atlas river flowing down towards the Marrakech plain. Our walks start from Tighdouine, a small town in the valley that we reach mid-morning after leaving Marrakech early.

There are no lines of hikers or souvenir shops here. The paths link villages, terraced fields and pastures, and most people you meet are locals heading to their fields or leading their animals.

Tlatast, the potters’ village

The first stop, and for many travellers the first highlight, is the old village of Tlatast and its pottery workshops. In earthen workshops, potters shape the clay with skills passed down through generations.

The pieces then dry in the sun, lined up along the walls, before being fired. Take your time to watch, ask questions through your guide and, if a piece catches your eye, buy it directly from the potter. Always ask before photographing the potters.

Staying with local families

In the evening we sleep with a family in the village of Azgour, near the Oued Zat. Comfort is simple: mattresses on the floor, shared rooms, basic bathrooms. But the welcome, the mint tea and the tagine shared from the same dish are worth more than any hotel.

It is also the moment to understand life in the valley: the harvest, the flocks, the children’s school, the water shared between the fields. Your guide translates, and conversations often go on long after dinner.

The Yagour plateau and its rock engravings

On the second day we climb to the Yagour plateau, a vast high plateau at roughly 2,300 to 2,700 metres. In summer, shepherds bring their flocks up to the high pastures, managed collectively under the rules of the agdal.

On the rock slabs you discover more than a thousand engravings: discs often interpreted as suns, geometric patterns, animals and human figures. Their exact age is debated by researchers; many link them to the Bronze Age, around three thousand years ago or more. What is certain is that people lived and grazed their animals here long before the Common Era.

These engravings are fragile, protected heritage. Do not touch them or walk on them, and never use chalk or water to make them stand out in a photo: the low light of early morning or late afternoon is enough to reveal them.

Crossing to Aït Inzal

After a night with a family or in camp, on the third day we cross to the opposite side, into the Aït Inzal area, for another night with a family in the village of Aït Ali or a nearby village. On the last day we walk back down to Tighdouine before driving back to Marrakech.

Who is it for?

This trek suits good walkers who prefer encounters to records. It does not go as high as Toubkal, but it needs stamina and a taste for simple comfort. The full day-by-day programme is on the 4-day Oued Zat valley trek page.

Before you go to the Oued Zat

  • Bring cash in dirhams: there are no cash machines in the villages.
  • Pack a warm sleeping bag and a head torch for homestay nights.
  • Dress simply, covering shoulders and knees in the villages.
  • Never touch the engravings or wet them to photograph them.
  • Keep room in your bag for a well-wrapped piece of Tlatast pottery.

Frequently asked questions

Are the engravings easy to see?

Yes, with a guide who knows the engraved slabs. In the morning and late afternoon, the low light makes them much easier to read.

Is homestay comfort very basic?

Yes: mattresses on the floor, shared rooms and basic bathrooms. In return, the welcome and the meals are unforgettable.

Is this trek suitable for beginners?

It suits fit people used to walking for several hours. It is less demanding than the Toubkal ascent, but it needs stamina.
